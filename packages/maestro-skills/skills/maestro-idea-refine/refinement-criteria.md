# Compare directions

## User value

Distinguish a painkiller from a vitamin. Acute, frequent pain, active workarounds and willingness to switch
are stronger evidence than polite approval. Name specific people with the problem, what they do today,
frequency, switching cost and whether demand comes from users or the builder's guess. A rare problem or
"everyone could use this" needs more evidence, not a broader feature list.

## Feasibility

Check technical reliability and the hardest unknown; external API/data dependencies; minimum stack, team and
specialist knowledge; legal/regulatory requirements; and time until a user can try it. Name the critical path.
A plan dependent on several unsolved research problems is not a small MVP.

## Differentiation

Ask what a user would tell a friend, whether the difference matters to that user and how durable it is.
Distinguish a new capability, a major improvement, a newly served audience or context, simpler experience
and lower price. Technical novelty without a user-visible benefit is weak evidence. Price alone is easy to copy.

## Assumption audit

Classify assumptions explicitly:

- Dealbreaker: if false, the idea fails; validate before building.
- Important: materially affects success but allows a changed approach.
- Optional: affects secondary features; defer validation until the core is proven.

Use the relevant value/feasibility quadrant. High value/high feasibility goes first; high value/low feasibility
may justify risk; low value/high feasibility is worthwhile only if trivial; low value/low feasibility is rejected.
Use differentiation to break ties inside a quadrant, not to excuse missing user value.

## Scope the MVP

Do one user job well. Test the riskiest assumption first. Ask what can be built and tested within the available
time rather than assembling every desired feature. Write a Not Doing list with reasons. An initial version
can feel incomplete to its builder; do not expand it just to make the feature list look finished.
